@f2_auftrag

@aufl_f2
This depends on your measurement. Often, with an open mug and $kA$ from the first 10 min, later points lie above the curves. Possible causes: the heat loss grows faster than linearly with $\vartheta - \vartheta_\mathrm{u}$ (evaporation, free convection), the room temperature is not right, a start-up effect when pouring (mug not fully preheated), the position of the thermometer, mugs that are not the same. Measurements to test this: a mug with a lid, the room temperature measured next to the mugs, the same surface under each mug, the measurement series in the “ln” view (going further). The difference between mug 1 and mug 2 often fits better than the individual values.

<p><strong>Try it: first predict, then check.</strong> Work on the questions as a pair. Write down your own idea of what happens first, then agree and lock in your prediction in the field. Only then check in the diagram. The explanation appears in the card once your prediction is locked in.</p>

@text_problem
<p>Sunday morning in a shared kitchen: you have made filter coffee and filled two identical cups with {{e:VK_ml}} ml each. Then the phone rings, and the call will last an hour. Your flatmate pours his milk into the coffee straight away (cup 1). You put your cup next to it and want to add the milk only after the call (cup 2), so that the coffee “stays hot for longer”. In both cases the milk comes straight from the fridge.</p>
<p>Your flatmate says: “It doesn’t matter whether you add the milk straight away or later. Energy is conserved, after all.” Which cup is warmer after an hour?</p>
<p>Both cups release heat to the room through their surface, and the more so the warmer the contents are compared with the room. The simulation calculates the process with the transient lumped energy balance from tutorial 5; when going further, you can change amounts, temperatures, cup and mug.</p>

@text_annahmen
<p>As on the problem sheet:</p>
<ul>
<li>The contents of the cup are perfectly mixed; the milk mixes in immediately and without heat loss.</li>
<li>The heat capacity of the cup is neglected, except when you switch the mug on (part g): then the mug is always at the temperature of its contents, also when the milk is poured in.</li>
<li>The total heat loss (including radiation and evaporation) is $\dot{Q}_\mathrm{ab} = kA\,(\vartheta - \vartheta_\mathrm{u})$ with a constant $kA$; the evaporated mass is neglected.</li>
<li>Coffee (properties of water) and milk are incompressible ($c_p = c_v$); the material properties are constant.</li>
</ul>
<p>In addition, in the simulation:</p>
<ul>
<li>$kA$ applies to both cups; “$kA$ of cup 1, relative” changes this for cup 1 only. When kept fixed, $kA$ stays the same when you change amounts or temperatures (here the filling level does not change $kA$); from the measurement, it is determined again every time.</li>
<li>Presets marked with a star are estimates, not measured; measured are “Ceramic, open” (problem) and “Ceramic with lid” (one mug with a polystyrene lid, Ferreira 2025a).</li>
<li>When the measured values are used, the mugs before the milk count as black coffee with the same initial value and the same $kA$ (mean of the measured values).</li>
<li>Perfect mixing and “mug at the temperature of its contents” are deliberately simplifying assumptions; the small Biot number of the ceramic wall (about {{v:biot}}) supports them, but does not prove them on its own.</li>
</ul>

@text_macht
<ol>
<li>It takes $kA$ of the cup: kept fixed (default {{z:kA}} $\frac{\mathrm{W}}{\mathrm{K}}$ from the measurement in the problem, a preset or your own value) or from the initial temperature and the measurement after 10 min (part c, with a mug part g).</li>
<li>Cup 1: it mixes coffee and milk at $t$ = 0 (part a) and lets the mixture cool down until the milk is added to cup 2 (parts b and d).</li>
<li>Cup 2: it lets the black coffee cool down until the milk is added and then mixes the milk in.</li>
<li>It compares both cups directly after the milk is added, calculates the heat released from the energy balance (part e) and the intersection of the curves.</li>
<li>It plots your measured values; if you want, it uses the initial temperature and the value after 10 min from the measurements or determines $kA$ from all points before the milk.</li>
</ol>
<p>It does not calculate: evaporation as a separate process (that is your homework), temperature differences within the cup, a heat loss that grows faster than linearly. All numbers are produced in your browser; the page sends nothing.</p>

@text_rechenweg
<p><strong>Balance.</strong> The control volume is the contents of one cup (with a mug: contents and mug). From the general energy balance (lecture slides 05, slide 8), without mass flows and without work, only the change in internal energy and the heat flow rate to the surroundings remain:</p>
$$C\,\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}t} = -kA\,(T - T_\mathrm{u})$$
<p>with $C = m\,c_p$ of the contents (with a mug, plus $m_\mathrm{B} c_\mathrm{B}$). Separation of variables with $T(t = 0\,\mathrm{s}) = T_0$ gives</p>
$$T(t) = T_\mathrm{u} + (T_0 - T_\mathrm{u}) \exp\left(-\frac{kA}{C}\,t\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{C}{kA}.$$
<p><strong>kA from the measurement.</strong> $kA = -\frac{C}{t_\mathrm{mess}} \ln\frac{T_\mathrm{mess} - T_\mathrm{u}}{T_0 - T_\mathrm{u}}$ (part c).</p>
<p><strong>Mixing.</strong> $\vartheta_\mathrm{misch} = \frac{m_\mathrm{K} c_{p,\mathrm{K}}\,\vartheta_\mathrm{K} + m_\mathrm{M} c_{p,\mathrm{M}}\,\vartheta_\mathrm{M}}{m_\mathrm{K} c_{p,\mathrm{K}} + m_\mathrm{M} c_{p,\mathrm{M}}}$ (part a; with a mug, its heat capacity is added to the cup).</p>
<p><strong>Heat released.</strong> $Q = C\,(\vartheta_\mathrm{Anfang} - \vartheta_\mathrm{Ende})$ for each control volume (part e); from this, directly after the milk is added, $C_\mathrm{G}(\vartheta_1 - \vartheta_2) = Q_2 - Q_1 + m_\mathrm{M} c_{p,\mathrm{M}}(\vartheta_\mathrm{M1} - \vartheta_\mathrm{M2})$; with the same milk, the last term vanishes.</p>
<p><strong>Time of adding.</strong> If the milk is added later, the difference changes by $\frac{\mathrm{d}(\vartheta_1 - \vartheta_2)}{\mathrm{d}t_\mathrm{Z}} = \frac{\dot{Q}_2 - \dot{Q}_1}{C_\mathrm{G}}$: it grows as long as cup 2 releases more heat, and decreases afterwards. What this means for the largest difference is in the explanations of question 1 and V2.</p>
<p><strong>kA from all measured points</strong> (going further): line through the origin in the “ln” view, slope $b = \sum t_i y_i / \sum t_i^2$ with $y_i = \ln\frac{\vartheta_i - \vartheta_\mathrm{u}}{\vartheta_0 - \vartheta_\mathrm{u}}$, then $kA = -C\,b$. The scatter about the line is not a measurement uncertainty of $kA$, because all points contain the same initial value.</p>
<p><strong>Keeping kA fixed</strong> (going further): the tool gives the model the measured value after 10 min that belongs to this $kA$ and otherwise calculates unchanged.</p>
